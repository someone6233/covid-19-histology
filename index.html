Step 2 · Meet the suspect

Meet the virus: SARS-CoV-2

SARS-CoV-2 is the virus that causes COVID-19. It is far too small to see with your eyes — about 1,000 of them could line up across the width of a human hair. Unlike a cell, a virus can't eat, grow, or reproduce on its own. It survives by breaking into a living cell and hijacking it to make copies of itself.

Real microscope image · NIAID-RML, colorized transmission electron micrograph (public domain / CC BY 2.0)

The bumps around the outside are called spike proteins. They work like a key: they fit into a matching "lock" — a receptor called ACE2 — on the surface of certain human cells, especially the cells lining our airway and lungs. That's how the virus gets inside.

Curriculum connection: Every cell has a cell membrane — a protective boundary that controls what goes in and out. The virus's whole strategy is about tricking that membrane into letting it through.

Step 4 · Build the bigger picture

From cells to systems

Cells don't work alone. They're organized into bigger and bigger teams:

Cell→ Tissue→ Organ→ Organ system
Tissue
A group of similar cells working together on the same job. Example: the thin cells that line the inside of your lungs.
Organ
Different tissues teaming up to do a bigger job. Example: your lung.
Organ system
Organs working together on one goal. Example: the respiratory system (nose, trachea, lungs) — getting oxygen into your body.
Alveolus
A tiny, thin-walled air sac at the very end of the airway, where oxygen actually crosses into the blood. Your lungs have millions of them.
Pneumocyte
The name for a cell that makes up the wall of an alveolus. There are two kinds, which you'll meet later: a thin flat kind (Type I) and a rounder kind (Type II).

    Step 9 · Run lab tests

    Virtual histology lab

    Plain slides are hard to read — almost everything looks pale pink or clear. That's why histologists use stains: special dyes that colour different parts of the tissue so they stand up under the microscope.

    Some stains are like a highlighter that only colours one specific thing — for example, a stain that lights up only virus particles and leaves everything else pale. Scientists call this an immune stain, because it uses the body's own immune-system tools (antibodies) to find its target. Keep that in mind — you'll use one later.
