Step 2 · Introduction: The Virus

Meet the Virus: SARS-CoV-2

SARS-CoV-2 is the virus that causes COVID-19. It is far too small to see with your eyes. Think of it this way: about 1,000 of them could line up across the width of a human hair. Viruses might look like cells and/or living things, but neither of these factoids are true. Unlike a cell, a virus can't eat, grow, or reproduce on its own. It survives by breaking into a living cell and hijacking it to make copies of itself. Basically, the all the virus wants to do is make more of itself.

Figure 1: Real microscope image of COVID-19 · CDC / Alissa Eckert, MSMI, Dan Higgins, MAMS (2020)

You've probably seen the image of COVID-19 above, but why does it look like that? The little protruding bumps around the outside are called spike proteins. They work like a key (or like enzymes solely in connection to the "lock-and-key" mechanism) they fit into a matching "lock". This is a receptor called ACE2, located on the surface of certain human cells, especially the cells lining our airway and lungs (keep this in mind!). That's how the virus gets inside your cells.

Why is this important for you? Every cell has a cell membrane, which acts as a protective boundary that controls what goes in and out. The virus's whole strategy is about tricking that membrane into letting it through.

Step 4 · A larger picture

Cellular Organization in a Nutshell

Cells are extraverts, they don't like to work alone. As such, they're generally organized into a hierarchy that grows as you move along it. Let's look below at this hierarchy and their definitions:

Cell→ Tissue→ Organ→ Organ system
Cell
The basic, structural, functional, and biological unit of all living organisms.
Tissue
A group of similar cells working together on the same function. Example: the cells that line the inside of your lungs.
Organ
Different tissues together working to perform a specific function. Example: your lungs.
Organ system
Organs working together to perform a goal. Example: the respiratory system.

Stain

A stain is a something that goes into tissue makes certain parts pretty and shiny so we can see them (I like shiny stuff). We need these because a slice of tissue is basically see through, so without a stain you can't see anything.

Hint: Histologists like to use multiple stains together. Most commonly, H&E is used. Hematoxylin turns the nucleus purple/blue. Eosin turns the rest of the cell pink (pretty).

If you need more help: they're kind of like highlighters to make something stand out more.

For us specifically: the purple dots and pink areas are what I want to show you on the lung slides later (wink, wink).

How thin does a slice need to be?

That's too thick (so sad). Light can't get through, so the view is a dark blur. Why don't you try sliding the thickness down?

Section

A section is an very, very thin slice of tissue. It's usually only about 4–5 micrometers (µm) thick. The tool that cuts it is called a microtome. To do this, the tissue is held in a block of wax so it doesn't deform while it's cut.

Another hint (I'm so generous): micro = tiny and -tome = cutter. Therefore, a microtome is a "tiny cutter."

There's no good analogy, it's just like a fancy knife.

For us: we need light to pass through the lung tissue so we can see the cells and alveoli walls.

Fixation

Fixation means preserving tissue with a chemical called a fixative, most often formalin (you don't need to memorize this). Once the tissue is taken out of the body, its own enzymes and microbes start breaking it down, so the fixative stops that and keeps the cells in place.

Yay, another hint: to fix something here means to hold it firmly in place (I'm sure you know this)

For more context: you could think of it like pressing a flower in a book so it keeps its shape.

For us specifically: fixing keeps the lung cells looking just like they did in the patient, which is obviously important.

Mount

To mount a section means to attach it to a glass slide. A drop of clear glue goes on, then a tiny, thin piece of glass called a cover slip is laid on top.

More hints: think of mounting something on a bullitin board. You're trying to attach something so it stays put and can be displayed.

For more context: it could also be like laminating a photo. It stays flat and it is protected from scratches.

For us specifically: the cover slip keeps the lung section flat so the microscope can focus on it (yay vision).

Biopsy (tissue sample)

A biopsy is when you remove a piece of tissue from a somebody to look at it. The piece we remove is called a sample or specimen.

Hint for the word: bio = life and -opsy = looking at. Therefore, a biopsy is "looking at living tissue."

For an example: think of it like tasting a spoonful of soup instead of eating the whole pot (idk I wrote this at 12am).

In our case: a small piece of the patient's lung lets us see what's happening inside without examining the entire organ.
