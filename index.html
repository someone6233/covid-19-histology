Step 2 · Introduction: The Virus

Meet the Virus: SARS-CoV-2

SARS-CoV-2 is the virus that causes COVID-19. It is far too small to see with your eyes. Think of it this way: about 1,000 of them could line up across the width of a human hair. Viruses might look like cells and/or living things, but neither of these factoids are true. Unlike a cell, a virus can't eat, grow, or reproduce on its own. It survives by breaking into a living cell and hijacking it to make copies of itself. Basically, the all the virus wants to do is make more of itself.

Figure 1: Real microscope image of COVID-19 · CDC / Alissa Eckert, MSMI, Dan Higgins, MAMS (2020)

You've probably seen the image of COVID-19 above, but why does it look like that? The little protruding bumps around the outside are called spike proteins. They work like a key (or enzymes solely in connection to the "lock-and-key" mechanism) they fit into a matching "lock". This is a receptor called ACE2, located on the surface of certain human cells, especially the cells lining our airway and lungs (keep this in mind!). That's how the virus gets inside your cells.

Why is this important for you? Every cell has a cell membrane, which acts as a protective boundary that controls what goes in and out. The virus's whole strategy is about tricking that membrane into letting it through.

Step 4 · A larger picture

From Cells to Systems

Cells don't work alone. They're organized into a hierarchy that grows as you move along it. Below lists this hierarchy and their definitions:

Cell→ Tissue→ Organ→ Organ system
Cell
The basic, structural, functional, and biological unit of all living organisms.
Tissue
A group of similar cells working together on the same job. Example: the thin cells that line the inside of your lungs.
Organ
Different tissues together working to perform a specific function. Example: your lungs.
Organ system
Organs working together to perform a goal. Example: the respiratory system.

    Step 9 · Run lab tests

    Virtual histology lab

    Plain slides are hard to read — almost everything looks pale pink or clear. That's why histologists use stains: special dyes that colour different parts of the tissue so they stand up under the microscope.

    Some stains are like a highlighter that only colours one specific thing — for example, a stain that lights up only virus particles and leaves everything else pale. Scientists call this an immune stain, because it uses the body's own immune-system tools (antibodies) to find its target. Keep that in mind — you'll use one later.
